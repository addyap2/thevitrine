On the French Riviera, some of your customers don't speak French, or would rather read in their own language. A multilingual website isn't a luxury for big companies: it's often the simplest way not to lose those visitors on the first page.

## Who it really helps

- **Restaurants and shops in tourist areas.** The [Ristorante da Lola](/en/work/ristorante-da-lola/) site is in Italian, English, French and German, so everyone reads the menu and opening hours in their own language.
- **Trades serving an international clientele.** [Plan B Concept](/en/work/plan-b-concept/) represents overseas owners through building projects, and its site speaks English and French.
- **Associations, institutions and training organisations** with varied audiences. Some of our learning platforms offer their interface in around ten languages.

If your customers already write to you in English, Italian or German, that's a good sign a version in their language would help them.

## The rules for getting it right

**1. A real translation, checked.** An unchecked machine translation shows immediately and costs trust. Tools help you go fast, but every version needs reading through.

**2. One page per language, with its own address.** Each version needs its own URL (for example `/en/` for English). That's what lets Google show the right version to the right visitor, using tags that link the pages together.

**3. A visible language switch.** The choice of language belongs at the top of the page, written in the target language ("Français", "Italiano"), not hidden in a menu.

**4. Don't trap visitors.** You can suggest the browser's language automatically, but visitors must always be able to switch, and their choice should be remembered. That's what this site does: an English-speaking visitor lands on the English version, but if they choose French, the site remembers.

**5. The same information everywhere.** Opening hours, prices and contact details must match in every language. A forgotten, out-of-date version does more harm than no version at all.

## Where to start

You don't have to translate everything at once. Start with the language your customers speak most after your main one, and with the pages that matter: the home page, the menu or services, opening hours and contact.
